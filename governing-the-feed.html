Governing the Feed: Misinformation and My Generation

Who would have thought that an innocuous comment made by the head of the highest judicial body of our country would lead to a revolution? Having likened unemployed youth to pests, I saw accounts by the name of @cockroachjantaparty both on X and Instagram the very next day in May, probably intended to be satire, but soon grew into a lot more: millions of sign-ups, an Instagram follower content that far exceeded those of well-established political parties in India, and the last month seeing lakhs of Indian youth on the streets of Central Delhi. While one could argue that it was a movement made for the internet and by the internet, it did something beyond the resignation of the Union Minister of Education – it also exposed the failures of the internet.

Misinformation refers to the false, inaccurate, or misleading information that is spread, most often without any intent to deceive or harm. While it is perceived to be a problem that can be solved by teaching online users, I believe the same is an internet governance issue. A malicious video that is fabricated can reach each of the 958 million active internet users in the country, and are also regulated by the Information Technology Act, 2000, as well as the takedown timelines and synthetic media disclosure requirements under the Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021. Apart from that, algorithms and reach play a huge role too. The spread of misinformation is not an accident of human nature; it is a design and regulatory choice.

The protests in Central Delhi made this concept even more concrete in the Indian perspective. For instance, a malicious deepfake that went viral depicted a Union Minister, who in the video was appearing to call for protesters to be publicly hanged. As a result, the Minister filed an FIR and as a result, the clip was traced to bad actors. A similar fabricated video depicted a senior Delhi police officer tendering his resignation in protest, as he was being asked by higher authorities to frame peaceful protestors. The common links to these videos were not just the fact that they were fake, but also that for days to follow, I received these videos from friends and relatives who believed that these sentiments were true and showed the opposing side of the protest in bad light. They believed that this footage was authentic.

As someone working in the digital rights space, what struck me was the symmetry of the damage. Fabricated videos vilified not only the State but also vilified Gen-Z and the protestors caused the same injury: to make verification feel futile. In my friend circles, despite the requirement of the law to label AI-generated content, there was a huge degree of mistrust. Real footage of the police action at Jantar Mantar was dismissed by some as AI. Inflated crowd videos let others dismiss the movement as manufactured. Political participation, which depends on a shared factual floor, was left unstable and led to withdrawal. Despite laws calling for provenance, and the lack of transparency and accountability on the internet may have led to momentum, but also called for the need to use independent verification and stricter labelling laws to ensure that the information environment remains healthy and that our generation continues to agitate while having access to correct information through proper internet governance.
