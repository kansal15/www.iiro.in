Cyberbullying and Intermediary Accountability

When we discuss cyberbullying, popular conversations usually fixate on individual actors: the aggressive bully behind a keyboard and the targeted victim enduring emotional distress. However, witnessing online harassment unfold within my own community has made me realize that individual behaviour is only half the story. The broader, systemic issue lies within internet governance—specifically the contentious debate surrounding intermediary accountability. We need to ask a fundamental question: how much responsibility should digital platforms like Instagram, TikTok, WhatsApp, or X carry when their infrastructure is weaponized to harass, doxx, or intimidate human beings?

Currently, most social media platforms operate under legal protections known as "safe harbour" provisions. Originally designed in the internet's early days, this legal framework treats tech corporations as neutral conduits—much like telecom operators who aren't held liable for what people say over phone calls. However, applying this outdated logic to modern social media feels fundamentally disconnected from reality.

Modern platforms are far from neutral pipelines. They rely on sophisticated, engagement-driven algorithms designed to amplify high-emotion content because outrage keeps users scrolling, viewing ads, and generating revenue. In my community, I have seen how quickly a targeted campaign of public shaming or deepfake defamation can escalate. The harm moves at lightning speed, leaving victims feeling helpless with no clear legal recourse, all while platforms actively profit from the viral engagement generated by the abuse.

Navigating this problem creates a complex tug-of-war among key stakeholders:

  • 1. Victims and Everyday Users: They urgently require safety, rapid content removal, clear reporting pathways, and meaningful legal accountability when severe harassment threatens their mental health or physical safety.
  • 2. Technology Intermediaries: Tech corporations argue that strict legal liability would make hosting user-generated content impossible, forcing them to spend billions on moderation or resort to aggressive automated censorship.
  • 3. Governments and Law Enforcement: Regulators struggle to enforce local protection laws against borderless tech giants, though their policy proposals often risk overreach, surveillance, and political control over public speech.
  • 4. Digital Rights Advocates: Civil liberty organizations caution that stripping away platform immunity will backfire, incentivizing platforms to censor marginal voices, dissenting opinions, and legitimate political debate.

Why does this governance challenge matter so deeply? Because we are caught in a genuine structural dilemma. On one side, maintaining total platform immunity leaves vulnerable individuals defenseless against life-altering online harm. On the other side, turning tech corporations into private judges and juries over public speech threatens the open, participatory nature of the web.

In my view, the current policy debate gets stuck in an unhelpful binary trap: either complete corporate immunity or heavy-handed state regulation. Platforms should not be allowed to enjoy the best of both worlds. If a company designs algorithms that intentionally favour outrage over user well-being to maximize profit, it cannot simultaneously claim to be a passive, neutral bulletin board when those same design choices enable severe harassment.

True intermediary accountability does not mean expecting platforms to censor every offensive comment, nor does it mean granting governments permission to monitor private conversations. Real accountability means holding tech companies responsible for their architectural choices. It means mandating algorithmic transparency, requiring responsive and human-driven reporting systems, enforcing default safety settings for younger users, and stripping away legal immunity when platforms turn a blind eye to verified, ongoing abuse. Cyberbullying is not merely a reflection of human malice; it is a direct consequence of a platform economy that prioritizes continuous engagement over human dignity.
